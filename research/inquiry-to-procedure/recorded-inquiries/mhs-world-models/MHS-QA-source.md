Q: Read https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/28/anthropic-proposes-plumbing-spec-to-link-ai-agents-to-lab-kit-and-robots/5293135?ref=aisecret.us. Is it analogous to a Digital Twin fully controlled by an AI agent?

A:This is a sharp conceptual question, and it's worth grounding it in Anthropic's own description rather than just the Register's (fairly snarky) summary. Let me pull the primary source.Short answer: not really — or only in a narrow sense that's easy to overstate. MHS is closer to the sensorimotor _plumbing_ a digital twin would sit on top of than to the twin itself.

Here's the reasoning.

What MHS actually is, stripped of the framing, is a standardized hardware-abstraction and control layer. It introduces a standardized driver with a simple set of primitives — "read" (e.g. get temperature) and "write" (e.g. set temperature) — makes each device discoverable in a common format, and produces a natural-language reference file describing each device's characteristics, what it can measure and adjust, and what safety limits to enforce. Agents then control the hardware through three paths: MCP, the command line, and code files. Its origin is telling: it grew out of a shared-memory "state dictionary" at Janelia that held an entire microscopy rig's state so the instruments could communicate at memory speed. So the thing is an integration bus + a live-state mirror + a control channel. "MCP for atoms," a hardware abstraction layer, a nervous system. It is not a model.

And that's the crux of why it isn't a digital twin. A twin's defining element is a persistent _virtual model_ of the asset — one you can simulate, predict from, and plan against, kept in sync with the physical counterpart. MHS has essentially none of that. It has a live state view and static device descriptors, but no simulation of dynamics. The world-model is deliberately offloaded to the LLM's implicit, text-and-image-derived understanding — which Anthropic itself flags as the weak point: Claude's understanding of a rig is "programmatic rather than physical," it lacks physical intuition, and in the Genentech work it couldn't recognize that foaming errors were physical failures rather than software bugs until it was told. A digital twin exists precisely to supply that physical/predictive model; MHS punts it to the agent.

Mapping onto the standard Kritzinger data-flow taxonomy: MHS does give you automatic physical→digital flow (state streaming via read) and digital→physical flow (agent-driven write), which is the bidirectional topology people associate with a _full_ twin rather than a mere model or shadow. But that taxonomy classifies the wiring, not the modeling. On the modeling axis, MHS tops out at digital-shadow grade — live state, no predictive layer.

The "fully controlled by an AI agent" half of your phrasing does fit: the agent closes the loop and drives the equipment. But notice it controls the _real_ instrument directly, not a virtual replica. One of the signature values of an actual twin is that you rehearse and test against the virtual copy before touching physical kit. MHS has no such sandbox — the agent experiments on the live rig, with MHS-enforced safety limits standing in for the safety that a simulate-first twin would provide. That's almost the inverse of the twin philosophy: minimal virtual intermediary, maximal direct action.

Where it _does_ resemble twin infrastructure, and where the analogy has some legs: that device reference file — arm weight, measurable quantities, adjustable ranges, enforced safety limits — is a nascent asset-information model, functionally similar to an Asset Administration Shell or the semantic layer of an Industry-4.0 twin. And MHS is a very plausible I/O substrate _beneath_ a real twin: stream its state dictionary into a simulation/predictive model and you'd have the twin proper, with MHS as the connective tissue. The Genentech and CMU closed loops (set a parameter, read the result back, score it, adjust) are a primitive digital-twin _pattern_ enacted at runtime — but the "model" in that loop is Claude reasoning ad hoc, not a maintained virtual replica.

So the precise verdict: analogous to the plumbing and the live-state shadow of an agent-controlled twin, not to the twin's defining virtual/predictive model. Anyone who labels the whole autonomous-lab setup "a digital twin controlled by an AI agent" is conflating the control/integration layer with the model layer — which, given your EUDR/monitoring work, is exactly the distinction worth keeping sharp, since a geospatial twin is almost all model and comparatively little actuation, whereas MHS is almost all actuation and interface with barely any model.

Q: Thus, the MHS is rather an additional layer that can be used in DT? Otherwise stated, using the Free Energy Principle, which can be used for defining the objectives of a DT, the MHS should be incorporated into the respective model.

A: Yes — and the Free Energy framing actually sharpens _where_ MHS sits, so let me push on it rather than just agree.

Take the active-inference partition of a system into internal states, external states, and the Markov blanket — sensory plus active states — that couples them. Map it onto the lab:

- External states — the physical instruments and the experiment: the hidden causes you can't observe directly.
- Markov blanket — MHS. Its read primitives are the sensory states; its write primitives are the active states. MHS _is_ the blanket, or rather the engineered realization of one.
- Internal states — the DT / generative model (with Claude currently standing in for much of it).

So MHS is not "incorporated into the model" as a _component of the generative model proper_; it's the interface across which model and world exchange influence. But your instinct is right in a more precise form: to run active inference, the generative model must contain a _model of the blanket_ — an action-conditioned likelihood and transition model saying "if I emit this write, the hidden state evolves thus and these reads will follow." That action-conditioned mapping is what has to be incorporated. And MHS conveniently hands you its scaffold: (cite index="2-1">the reference file and tags describing adjustable ranges, measurable quantities, and enforced safety limits</cite> are effectively a partial specification of the action and observation spaces — a schema for the likelihood you'd otherwise hand-build.

Then define the DT's objective as expected free energy, and two things from the Anthropic writeups fall out of a single functional. Pragmatic value (reaching preferred observations, where the prior preference is the scientist's stated goal) is the control half — steer the instruments to the target state: locked laser, accurate transfer, acceptable dose-response fit. Epistemic value (information gain, uncertainty reduction) is the exploratory half — and it's exactly what those "act, observe, adjust, repeat" loops are: (cite index="2-1">Claude nudging the laser and reading the camera to see how the beam moved, sweeping flow rates by trial transfer, rejecting a saturated curve and rerunning on a compressed concentration range.</cite> Under expected free energy those aren't heuristics; they're uncertainty-resolving actions the objective actively selects for.

That unification is the whole appeal: keeping the twin synced to the physical asset (data assimilation) becomes perceptual inference, and driving the asset to preferred states becomes active inference — both minimizing the same free energy. It's cleaner than the usual DT architecture of a calibration loop bolted to a separate optimizer.

Two further correspondences are worth noting because MHS already exhibits them. First, its device-level safety limits map to hard constraints on the admissible policy space — or, equivalently, near-infinite-precision priors ruling out certain states; the (cite index="2-1">agent pausing overnight for human confirmation before anything it deemed slightly risky</cite> is just very tight priors over risky policies. Second, (cite index="2-1">the deterministic scripts the agents distilled — the relock controller, the reusable liquid-handling skills — that then run without the model in the loop</cite> are policy compression, i.e. habit formation: once the posterior over the best policy is sharp enough, you compile it into a fixed controller and drop online inference. Active inference predicts precisely that migration from deliberative to habitual control as uncertainty collapses.

The honest caveat is really a restatement of the "almost no model" point from before. Incorporating MHS's read/write semantics into the generative model is the easy part — it's well-specified and self-describing. What FEP throws into relief is the hard part: the generative model of the _physics itself_, the transition dynamics with calibrated uncertainty, which MHS does not provide and which is currently being approximated by (cite index="2-1">the LLM's implicit, text-and-image-derived knowledge — no proper posterior, no calibrated uncertainty, and weak on exactly the physical constraints Anthropic flagged, like the foaming it misread as a software bug.</cite> So under a strict reading: MHS gives you the blanket and a schema for the likelihood over the blanket; it gives you essentially none of the internal generative model that makes free-energy minimization meaningful. The interface problem is solved. The modelling problem — where a digital twin actually lives — is untouched, and that's the layer you'd be building.

Q: Based on this, the text below, arguing that the invention of the MHS eliminates the need to build world models, is incorrect. "The idea was that an AI needs an internal simulation of physics, space, and causality to interact with the real world. Anthropic just proved that it does not. [Why build a world model](https://aisecret.us/r/ccfa4e33?m=21128895-a690-4507-8506-21d6848dcb1c) when you can plug into the world directly? MHS gives Claude a real microscope, a real robotic arm, a real laser. It does not need to simulate physics. It can just try things and watch what happens. The physical world is the simulator. The startups that bet their valuation on world models just got a new competitor they cannot beat: reality itself."

A: Your read is correct, and it's worth being exact about _why_, because the passage isn't just loosely wrong — it commits a specific category error and then dresses a very old, much narrower idea up as a new refutation.

Start with the kernel of truth, because there is one. "The world is its own best model" is Rodney Brooks' line from behaviour-based robotics, and the anti-representationalist tradition (embodied cognition, subsumption architecture) has real force: in tightly-scoped, well-instrumented, cheap-to-fail settings, you can substitute fast closed-loop correction for an elaborate precomputed simulator. So the defensible claim in the passage is: _you may not need a separate, high-fidelity, a-priori physics simulator as a standalone artifact._ Fine. But that is a claim about the **fidelity and locus** of the model — implicit and online versus explicit and offline — not about its **existence**. The passage smuggles the second, much stronger claim in under cover of the first.

And the stronger claim collapses immediately, on the argument we already built. "It can just try things and watch what happens" is not model-free — it is a description of model-based active inference. To _choose_ what to try you need a generative model that predicts which action will move the hidden state or reduce your uncertainty; to _learn from watching_ you need that same model to generate the prediction error you're correcting against. The epistemic value that makes "try and watch" intelligent rather than flailing is _defined relative to a generative model_ — "informative" has no meaning without a posterior to sharpen. Strip the world model out entirely and "watch what happens" degenerates into random actuation with no way to interpret the result. The passage has described model-based inference and labelled it the abolition of models.

Worse for the argument, Anthropic's own writeup is evidence _against_ the reading projected onto it. When Claude hit the foaming error it retried in the same well and made _more_ bubbles, and (cite index="2-1">it only recovered once a human told it the failure was physical and it needed to move to a clean well and reduce mixing cycles.</cite> Reality-as-simulator did not rescue it there — a human's causal model did. That's a world-modelling failure patched by injecting a world model from outside. Anthropic names the bottleneck explicitly: (cite index="2-1">Claude's understanding of the rig is "programmatic rather than physical," it lacks physical intuition, and its spatial and physical reasoning still require expert oversight.</cite> If reality were an adequate substitute for an internal model, that would not be _the_ limiting factor. "Anthropic just proved it does not" inverts what Anthropic actually said.

Then there's the economics, which the closing line gets backwards. Reality is the _most_ expensive, slowest, least parallel, least reversible, most partially-observable simulator available. A simulator's entire value is cheap, safe, resettable, fast-forwardable, massively parallel counterfactual rollout — reality offers none of these. You cannot un-destroy a sample, un-crash an objective lens, un-unlock a laser mid-computation, or (the article's own uranium joke) un-spin a centrifuge. This is precisely why the agents in the writeup didn't leave everything to live trial-and-error: they (cite index="2-1">distilled deterministic scripts — the relock controller, reusable liquid-handling skills — that then run without the model in the loop,</cite> and the labs are moving toward (cite index="2-1">custom predictive models that adaptively optimise experiments and compiler heuristics refined from measured data.</cite> The trajectory is toward _more_ modelling, not less — compress the expensive real interaction into cheap reusable structure the moment you can.

And the deepest point: Claude _already is_ a world model — a large, learned, if miscalibrated one, built from text and images. MHS didn't eliminate a world model; it connected an existing one to actuators. So the framing of world-model startups versus "reality itself" as substitutes is exactly wrong — they're complements. A clean, standardized interface to real instruments is the best thing that could happen to a world-model business, because it's a firehose of grounded interaction data to _train and calibrate_ the model against. MHS raises the value of good world models; it doesn't zero it.

For your own domain this is starkest of all. "The physical world is the simulator" presupposes you can act, observe, and iterate cheaply — which is unavailable exactly where digital twins earn their keep: irreversible, slow, high-stakes, expensive-to-observe systems. You cannot A/B-test a deforestation policy on the actual Amazon and roll back the failed arm. An environmental or EUDR-style twin is almost entirely generative model and counterfactual simulation, with barely any actuation channel — the polar opposite of MHS, and the case where "just try it in reality" is not merely inefficient but structurally impossible.

So the verdict: the passage conflates the Markov blanket with the generative model. MHS dissolves the _integration and actuation_ barrier — a real achievement — and the author mistakes that for dissolving the _prediction and understanding_ problem, which sits one layer in and is untouched. The honest one-line correction is that MHS doesn't remove the need for a world model; it removes the need to _hand-build the plumbing between_ a world model and the world — and in doing so makes the world model more valuable, not obsolete.
