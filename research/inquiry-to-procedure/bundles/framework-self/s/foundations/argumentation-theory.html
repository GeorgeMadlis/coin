Summary

Dung 1995 models argumentation through abstract frameworks of arguments and attacks. Dialogue-game approaches add procedural structure for exchanges among participants and should not be reduced to adversarial attack alone: Walton and Krabbe 1995 distinguish persuasion, inquiry, negotiation, information-seeking, deliberation, and eristic dialogue. These tools clarify both contested acceptability and non-adversarial inquiry moves.

What it explains

It explains how objections and counter-objections affect the acceptability of claims, and how different dialogue types can structure information-seeking, inquiry, deliberation, and commitment revision.

What it fails to explain

It can treat acceptance statically and may not preserve the lossy record of the dialogue itself as a first-class object. It also does not by itself distinguish a costly frame dispute from a dispute caused by facts absent from the surviving record.

What this framework adds

This framework adds the explicit recording function as a core requirement: the dialogue's history is itself recorded, pinned, and superseded by round. It also adds the computational-boundedness dimension for frame translation across participants and maps dialogue moves to stages: information-seeking to enrichment, critical probing to critique, live opposition to contestation, and agreed commitment or verdict change to consolidation.

Classification

Addresses Type I and Type II in both static and sequential regimes. It covers enrichment, critique, contestation, and consolidation more directly when dialogue-game literature is included. Type III requires this framework's additional recording-function account.
