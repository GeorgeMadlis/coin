Observer-Distance Metrics

This document fills the Step 5 deliverable. It defines measurable proxies for observer distance and trajectory behavior in the statement-evolution framework. It separates four things that should not be collapsed:

  1. Mathematical definitions: quantities over observers, records, rounds, stages, types, descriptions, and provenance fields.
  2. Measurement protocols: reproducible procedures that turn a recorded bundle or experiment into values.
  3. Repository-derived measurements: values computed from recorded files in bundles/framework-self/.
  4. Speculative hypotheses: expected relationships to P1-P5 and P4' in docs/framework_v3.md, not established empirical results.

Status note: the metrics are operational proposals. They do not validate the framework by themselves. A single self-referential trajectory demonstrates that measurements can be recorded and reproduced; it is not evidence that the framework's general propositions hold.

1. Measurement Vocabulary

Let R be the surviving record, O_i = (C_i, f_i) an observer, and y_i = f_i(R cap C_i) the observer's interpretation. Let a statement trajectory have rounds t = 1..n, stage sequence s_t, and contestation-only type sequence k_t for rounds where s_t = contestation.

For static cross-sections, metrics compare two or more observers at one time. For multi-observer trajectories, they compare parties in one interleaved round sequence. For diagonal trajectories, they compare time slices of one observer or one bundle state to its earlier state.

The word "distance" is always proxy language. None of the metrics measures a literal location in Wolfram's rulial space. Each proxy can reproduce the evaluator's own frame if the evaluator chooses labels, encodings, disclosure levels, or scoring rules that favor its preferred compression function.

2. Metric Families

The table below gives the required fields for every metric: formal or operational definition, unit and range, required inputs, measurement procedure, missing-data treatment, interpretation, confounders, failure modes, expected relationship to the framework propositions, falsification use, and trajectory applicability.

2.1 Observer-Distance Proxies

id metric definition unit and range required inputs measurement procedure missing data interpretation confounders failure modes expected relationship falsification use applies
OD-1 Predicted-continuation divergence Distance between observers' probability distributions or ranked predictions over the next h events in a shared stream: e.g. Jensen-Shannon divergence for distributions, Kendall distance for rankings, or Brier-score difference after outcomes are known. Normalized score in [0, 1] where 0 means identical predictions under the chosen scoring rule. Same event prefix, recorded predictions by each observer, horizon h, scoring rule, and later outcomes when scoring accuracy. Freeze the common prefix, collect predictions before later rounds, normalize output vocabulary, compute divergence, then optionally score against realized continuations. NOT MEASURABLE unless predictions are timestamped before outcomes and use comparable output vocabularies. Large divergence suggests different frames, priors, or context windows even before explicit disagreement appears. Prediction formats, horizon choice, calibration skill, strategic prediction, and output vocabulary. Observers may agree on predictions while disagreeing on meaning; or may diverge because one prediction task was underspecified. P5 predicts genuine trajectories often cannot be shortcut. Strong repeated ability to predict convergence and future rounds without running them would weaken P5. Static, multi-observer, diagonal.
OD-2 Forced-classification disagreement rate Fraction of shared items on which observers assign different labels under a forced shared codebook. Rate in [0, 1]; optionally Cohen's kappa or Krippendorff's alpha. Shared item set, codebook, at least two independent labels per item, adjudication policy. Give all observers the same items and label options; compute pairwise disagreement and, if useful, chance-corrected agreement. NOT MEASURABLE if labels are not independent, items differ, or the codebook was changed mid-run without versioning. High rate after data sharing suggests frame or category divergence. Bad codebook, ambiguous items, label imbalance, training effects, and social pressure. Forced labels can hide disagreement by forcing incompatible frames into one codebook. If raw-data sharing repeatedly reduces disagreement to near zero, Type I is supported. If disagreement persists under shared items and stable labels, Type I-only explanations are weakened. Static, multi-observer, diagonal if one observer re-labels later without seeing prior labels.
OD-3 Description-length asymmetry Difference or ratio between lengths of observers' descriptions of the same record under a fixed encoding and task: abs(L_i - L_j) or max(L_i,L_j)/min(L_i,L_j). Tokens, bytes, words, compressed bytes, or ratio >= 1. Same prompt/task, same source record, encoding rule, observer outputs. Freeze representation, collect descriptions, compute length, optionally compress with a declared compressor and settings. NOT MEASURABLE if the source prompt, encoding, or output constraints differ. Mark units and representation explicitly. Asymmetry may indicate that one frame treats the record as simpler or more natural than another. Language, verbosity norms, compression algorithm, prompt wording, audience, and task scope. Length can measure style rather than explanatory compression. If frame distance is claimed but all representation-stable description and classification measures show no divergence across many cases, the Type II diagnosis is weakened. Static, multi-observer, diagonal.
OD-4 Provenance completeness of R Fraction or checklist of required provenance fields present for deciding a target claim: source identity, version, timestamp, chain of custody, transformation steps, environment, checksums, and recorder specification. Component score in [0, 1], plus named missing fields. Target claim, required provenance schema, surviving records, reproduction axis or equivalent metadata. Define the claim-specific provenance checklist before scoring; mark each component present, absent, partial, or not applicable; report score and missing fields. Missing components are visible as NOT AVAILABLE, UNVERIFIED, or NOT MEASURABLE; do not impute. Low completeness raises risk that an apparent Type II frame dispute is actually Type III underdetermination. High completeness can rule out some Type III paths for the recorded claim. Checklist design, false confidence in metadata, irrelevant provenance fields, and inaccessible external systems. A high score can still miss the one absent fact that matters; a low score does not prove Type III. P3 predicts lost recorder specifications cause Type III to be misclassified as Type II. If low provenance never predicts persistence or underdetermination across cases, P3 is weakened. Static, multi-observer, diagonal.
OD-5 Recoverability of surviving record R Ability of a later observer to retrieve, verify, and rerun or re-derive the record needed by the current claim. Ordered levels: 0 absent, 1 cited but unpinned, 2 pinned but not re-runnable, 3 re-runnable with cost limits, 4 fully re-derived in current environment. Source links, commits, checksums, data versions, environment, commands, access permissions. Attempt retrieval and verification from a fresh environment; record the highest achieved level and the blocking reason. Use NOT AVAILABLE for absent sources, UNVERIFIED for unchecked pins, and computational vs factual irreducibility labels for blockers. Higher recoverability should reduce future Type I and Type III disputes about the trajectory's own history. Permissions, bit rot, proprietary data, cost, environment drift, and stale derived artifacts. A successful rerun may only reproduce the recorded computation, not the historical event being studied. If recoverable records still generate Type III disputes about facts supposedly pinned by the record, the checklist or Type III diagnosis is wrong. Static, multi-observer, diagonal.
OD-6 Deepest common disclosure level Deepest level L in {0,1,2,3} at which observers' accounts are equivalent before diverging. Level 0-3, or none, plus axis. Higher means more shared detail before divergence. Levelled accounts by observer or party, equivalence criterion, axis map. Compare accounts at L0, then L1, L2, and L3; record the deepest level still equivalent and the first divergent level. NOT MEASURABLE without parallel level-specific accounts or a declared equivalence criterion. Divergence at shallower levels signals broader frame or verdict disagreement; divergence only at deeper levels localizes dispute. Level design, summarizer bias, unequal detail, and weak equivalence rules. If shallow summaries are forced to agree, DCL can overstate agreement. If DCL does not help distinguish Type I, II, and III localization over many recorded disputes, the bundle-localization claim is weakened. Static, multi-observer, diagonal.

2.2 Contestation-Type Trajectory Metrics

Type metrics apply only to contestation-stage rounds. They must not be mixed with stage metrics.

For numeric type ordering, use I = 1, II = 2, III = 3, and omit resolved from descent calculations except as a closure marker. This ordering is not a value judgment; it encodes the framework's proposed descent from context-window gaps to frame gaps to absent-record residues.

id metric definition unit and range required inputs measurement procedure missing data interpretation confounders failure modes expected relationship falsification use applies
TT-1 Type descent rate Mean positive change in type depth per adjacent contestation round, or count of I->II, II->III, I->III transitions divided by contestation transitions. Rate in [0, 1] for transition share; depth change in {0,1,2} per transition. At least two contestation rounds with fc-type-at-round. Extract contestation rounds, map types to depth, count transitions where depth increases. NOT MEASURABLE with fewer than two contestation rounds or inferred types lacking rationale. Positive descent is consistent with Type I components resolving before Type II and Type III residues. Coarse labels, mixed-type rounds, adversarial moves, and changing classification standards. Labels may be post-hoc rationalizations rather than measured type movement. Many truthful, recorded contestations that move upward or never expose deeper residues would weaken P4. Multi-observer, diagonal contestation stretches.
TT-2 Type stalls Adjacent contestation transitions where type depth is unchanged while evidence or frame moves continue. Count and share of contestation transitions. Contestation type sequence and evidence/frame move markers. Count repeated type labels across adjacent contestation rounds with non-empty moves. NOT MEASURABLE with fewer than two contestation rounds. A stall can mean genuine difficulty, insufficient resources, or misclassification. Long-running hard Type II translation, strategic delay, evidence flooding. A stall is not by itself proof of Type III. Under continued evidence sharing, a persistent Type I stall should trigger misclassification review per P3. Multi-observer, diagonal contestation stretches.
TT-3 Type reversals Adjacent contestation transitions where type depth decreases, e.g. III->II or II->I. Count and share of contestation transitions. Contestation type sequence. Map type depth and count negative adjacent changes. NOT MEASURABLE with fewer than two contestation rounds. Reversal may reveal newly discovered records, earlier over-classification, or non-monotone/adversarial moves. Later evidence can legitimately re-open Type I components; type labels may mix subclaims. Treating every reversal as failure ignores multi-component disputes. Frequent reversals under truthful, recorded, single-claim contestations would weaken a monotone P4 reading. Multi-observer, diagonal contestation stretches.
TT-4 Type oscillations Alternation pattern in contestation types, such as I,II,I or II,III,II, over a window of length at least three. Count and oscillation share per eligible windows. At least three contestation rounds. Slide a three-round window over contestation rounds and count return-to-prior-depth patterns. NOT MEASURABLE with fewer than three contestation rounds. Oscillation indicates frame churn, unstable labeling, mixed claims, or adversarial dynamics. Over-broad statements, multiple live subclaims, and changing parties. A real multi-component dispute can look oscillatory if not decomposed. High oscillation in well-scoped, truthful records would weaken P4 and motivate Step 8 limitations. Multi-observer, diagonal contestation stretches.

2.3 Stage-Trajectory Metrics

Stage metrics describe the whole statement trajectory. They are distinct from contestation type metrics because most rounds have no live counter-position.

id metric definition unit and range required inputs measurement procedure missing data interpretation confounders failure modes expected relationship falsification use applies
ST-1 Stage-transition rate Number of adjacent round pairs where s_t != s_{t+1} divided by n-1. Rate in [0, 1]. Complete stage sequence. Extract fc-stage from round files or a documented retrospective index; count adjacent changes. If historical stages are back-assigned, label them inferred and report frontmatter coverage separately. Higher rate means the statement is moving among lifecycle roles; lower rate means long stretches of one stage. Stage taxonomy granularity, retroactive classification, and merged multi-purpose rounds. High transition rate can reflect bookkeeping style, not inquiry dynamics. If stage movement never correlates with consolidation or supersession outcomes, P4' stage hypotheses are weakened. Multi-observer, diagonal.
ST-2 Time to first contestation First contestation round minus formation round; optionally calendar duration. Rounds and elapsed time; NOT OBSERVED if none. Stage sequence and timestamps. Find first formation and first later contestation; subtract round numbers and dates. NOT OBSERVED is distinct from missing data; NOT MEASURABLE if stages or dates are missing. Shorter time means disagreement appears early; no contestation means the trajectory has not entered observer disagreement. Missing early rounds, retrospective stage labels, and bundled changes. Absence of contestation may mean no rival observer, not consensus. If critique-before-contestation does not reduce later contestation length across cases, Step 6 prediction 8 weakens. Multi-observer, diagonal.
ST-3 Contestation round count Number of rounds with fc-stage: contestation. Non-negative integer and share of rounds. Stage sequence. Count contestation rounds. NOT MEASURABLE if stage sequence absent. Larger counts indicate longer live disagreement stretches. Over-splitting or merging rounds, mixed-stage records. Count ignores severity and content of disagreement. If many contestations resolve without type descent, P4 needs revision or narrower assumptions. Multi-observer, diagonal.
ST-4 Time or rounds to consolidation First consolidation round after formation or after first contestation; optionally all consolidation intervals. Rounds and elapsed time; NOT OBSERVED if none. Stage sequence and timestamps. Compute interval from formation to first consolidation and from first contestation to next consolidation. NOT OBSERVED if no consolidation; NOT MEASURABLE if stages or dates missing. Shorter intervals may indicate productive inquiry or premature closure. Administrative consolidations, low-stakes changes, and conventional rather than evidential settlement. Consolidation is supersedable; it is not proof of truth. If consolidation behaves as a permanent sink, that would contradict the framework's own supersession caveat, not validate it. Multi-observer, diagonal.
ST-5 Post-consolidation supersession rate Share of rounds after first consolidation that supersede at least one current concept; optionally number of superseded concepts per post-consolidation round. Rate in [0,1]; count per round. First consolidation round, concept frontmatter, supersession metadata, round list. Identify first consolidation; for later rounds, count current concept files whose fc-round equals that round and have fc-supersedes. NOT MEASURABLE if supersession metadata absent or old versions cannot be identified. Measures how often consolidated material is later revised. Metadata repairs, broad concept files, and current-state-only visibility. Current files hide superseded successors unless git history is queried. If post-consolidation supersession is common even after critique-before-contestation, Step 6 prediction 8 weakens. Multi-observer, diagonal.
ST-6 Re-litigation rate Share of post-consolidation rounds that reopen the same settled claim, issue, or concept without adding new evidence or frame rationale. Rate in [0,1]; requires issue identifiers. Stable claim/issue ids, consolidation markers, later touches, evidence/frame rationale. Track each consolidated issue; count later contestation or critique rounds over the same issue with no new basis. NOT MEASURABLE without stable issue ids or explicit "same point" metadata. High rate suggests weak recording discipline or premature consolidation. Legitimate reopening, new evidence, and concept files that contain many subclaims. Without issue ids, this becomes evaluator judgment. If disciplined bundles do not lower re-litigation relative to undocumented inquiries, prediction 7 weakens. Multi-observer, diagonal.
ST-7 Evidence added per round Count of evidence moves, evidence links, or evidence items per round under a declared counting rule. Items per round, or evidence-move share in [0,1]. Round records and evidence-item extraction rule. Count fc-move: evidence or parse # Evidence added items; report the rule used. Use NOT MEASURABLE for item counts when evidence is prose-only or mixed with implementation changes. Higher rate suggests context-window growth; in contestation, persistent disagreement despite evidence can expose Type II/III. Large items vs small items, infrastructure evidence, and duplicate links. Counting bullets can reward verbosity or split artifacts. If continued evidence sharing resolves all disputes regardless of frame/provenance, Type II/III claims weaken. Multi-observer, diagonal.
ST-8 Frame-change frequency Number of fc-move: frame-change rounds divided by total rounds; optionally count consolidation frame adoptions separately from contestation frame splits. Rate in [0,1]. Round fc-move and fc-stage. Count frame-change moves and classify their stage. NOT MEASURABLE if move metadata missing. Higher frequency suggests changing compression functions or policy frames. Some frame changes are administrative clarifications; others are substantive. Move labels may undercount frame changes hidden in evidence rounds. If frame-change frequency has no relationship to persistence after data sharing, Type II operationalization weakens. Multi-observer, diagonal.
ST-9 Observer or agent handoff frequency Number of adjacent round pairs where fc-party changes divided by n-1. Rate in [0,1]. Round party sequence. Extract fc-party and count adjacent changes. NOT MEASURABLE if party metadata missing or unstable. Higher rate means more sequential observer handoffs; it can expose multi-observer differences. Party labels may conflate human and tool; long single-party stretches may still include hidden reviewers. Handoff count does not measure substantive disagreement. If handoffs systematically add no new critiques, frames, or provenance changes, the sequential-observer contribution may be weaker than claimed. Multi-observer; diagonal when parties are time slices only if explicitly encoded.

3. Protocol Notes and Boundary Cases

3.1 Provenance Completeness, Type II, and Type III

Provenance completeness cannot by itself distinguish Type II from Type III. It can change the diagnostic odds:

  • Low or missing provenance raises Type III risk because the disputed content or recorder specification may be absent from R.
  • High provenance can rule out some Type III explanations for a specific claim, because later observers can inspect the relevant record and how it was produced.
  • High provenance does not eliminate Type II. Two observers can inspect the same complete record and still apply different frames.
  • Low provenance does not prove Type III. The missing field may be irrelevant to the disputed proposition.

Therefore OD-4 is a diagnostic proxy, not a classifier. A Type III diagnosis still requires showing that the disputed proposition is underdetermined by the surviving record.

3.2 Stalled Type Trajectories

A stalled Type I trajectory under continued evidence sharing is a warning sign, not an automatic reclassification. Under P3, the likely explanations include:

  • the apparent missing data is not actually present in R, making the case Type III;
  • the observers share data but compress it differently, making the residual Type II;
  • the evidence sharing is incomplete, untruthful, or not recorded; or
  • the statement combines multiple subclaims that should be split.

The measurement protocol should trigger a classification review and record the reason for either retaining the Type I label or superseding it.

3.3 Description Length Is Representation-Dependent

Description length depends on the representation: language, encoding, token scheme, prompt, audience, allowed notation, and compression algorithm. A short Python program, a long plain-English paragraph, and a table may describe the same record with different byte counts. OD-3 is therefore only meaningful under a fixed measurement representation. It cannot be treated as an observer- independent measure of understanding.

3.4 Kolmogorov Complexity Is Not Directly Measurable

Kolmogorov complexity is the length of the shortest program that generates a string on a fixed universal machine. It is not computable in general. This framework therefore never claims to measure true Kolmogorov complexity. Description-length metrics are practical proxies such as word count, token count, MDL-style model cost, or compressed bytes under a declared compressor. Those proxies are useful only when their representation and compressor are explicitly recorded.

3.5 Evaluator-Frame Reproduction

Metric choice can reproduce the evaluator's own frame. Examples:

  • a forced-classification codebook can make one observer's categories the only legal categories;
  • a disclosure-level comparison can privilege the bundle author's hierarchy;
  • a provenance checklist can overvalue fields familiar to one discipline;
  • a description-length measure can reward the evaluator's preferred language or notation.

Mitigations: publish codebooks before labeling, allow objections to metric choice as critique or contestation rounds, record missing values, and preserve alternative encodings when they materially change the result.

5. Hypotheses for Later Testing

These are speculative hypotheses, not repository results:

  1. Sharing raw data reduces OD-2 disagreement for Type I components more than for Type II or Type III components.
  2. OD-4 and OD-5 predict Type III risk but do not independently classify Type III. The key test is persistence under improved record sharing and frame translation.
  3. Persistent TT-2 stalls under continued evidence sharing predict earlier misclassification, especially Type III hidden as Type II under P3.
  4. Higher OD-6 deepest common disclosure level should localize disputes more precisely and reduce unnecessary frame-level contestation.
  5. High ST-6 re-litigation should be lower in disciplined bundles than in undocumented inquiry chains of comparable depth.
  6. A critique-stage round before first contestation should correlate with lower ST-3 contestation count and lower ST-5 post-consolidation supersession rate.

Each hypothesis requires multiple bundles or external case studies. The framework-self pilot alone validates none of them.
