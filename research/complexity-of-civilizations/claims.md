# Claims — Complexity of Civilizations

Each claim is prefixed with the attributed author, or `[unsourced]` if no author is named.

## Core thesis

- **Tainter**: Civilizations collapse due to diminishing marginal returns on complexity, not from barbarians, drought, plague, earthquakes, or resource depletion.
- **Tainter**: He tested 11 conventional collapse explanations across 17 civilizations on 6 continents over 5,000 years and every one failed.
- **Tainter**: Societies are problem-solving organisations; early investments in complexity yield high returns that steadily decline until each additional unit costs more than it returns.
- **Tainter**: Collapse is not catastrophe — it is an economising process, a civilisation shedding complexity it can no longer afford.
- **Tainter**: The Roman barbarians did not suddenly get stronger; Rome got weaker because of its own complexity burden.
- **Tainter**: Modern civilisation is globally interconnected, so collapse will not be local — world civilisation will collapse as a whole.

## Roman case study

- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: The Roman Republic's legal code in 451 BC consisted of the 12 Tables — approximately 140 provisions, short enough for schoolboys to memorise.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: Emperor Justinian's 534 AD legal consolidation compressed 3 million lines of accumulated legal text into ~150,000 lines (the *Digest*), drawing on 38 separate jurists.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: The *Codex Justinianus* contained 4,562 individual statutes — a 33× multiplication of the legal code across one millennium.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: The *Theodosian Code* (438 AD) already held over 2,500 constitutions before Justinian.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: Under Augustus (~27 BC) Rome administered ~25–28 provinces with ~15,000 civil servants.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: Under Diocletian (284 AD), provinces doubled to ~100, organised into 12 dioceses and 4 prefectures; civil service roughly doubled to ~30,000.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: Lactantius wrote that under Diocletian "there were now more men using tax money than there were paying it."
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: Diocletian's Edict on Maximum Prices (301 AD) set ceilings on over 1,200 goods, services, and wages with a death penalty for violation — and was universally ignored within 4 years.
- **Tainter**: The silver content of the Roman denarius fell from ~95% under Augustus to ~75% under Marcus Aurelius, below 60% under Septimius Severus, and to 2–5% under Gallienus (~269 AD).
- **Tainter**: The debasement curve matches the shape of the marginal returns graph almost perfectly.

## Ottoman case study

- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: In 1365, Murad I administered two provinces with an Imperial Council of three viziers.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: By the late 1500s, the Ottoman Empire governed 42 provinces with 11 viziers.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: Central bureaucracy grew from 38 salaried scribes in 1549 to ~1,500 by the 1790s — a 40× increase in administrative staff in 250 years.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: The Janissary Corps began at ~2,000 elite soldiers; by 1808 it had swelled to 140,000, of whom only ~10% were trained soldiers — the rest had purchased enrollment for tax exemptions.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: In 1810 alone Janissaries set over 2,000 arson fires in Constantinople and demanded bribes to extinguish them.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: Sultan Mahmud II surrounded Janissary barracks with artillery in 1826 and killed over 6,000 in a single day (the "Auspicious Incident").
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: At its peak, the Ottoman Empire operated 4–5 parallel legal jurisdictions simultaneously: Sharia courts, secular Kanun courts, Millet courts, capitulatory courts, and post-Tanzimat secular state courts.
- **Tainter / [unsourced]**: The *Mecelle* codification (1869–1876) produced 1,851 articles across 16 volumes — covering only civil law, not family law.

## Mancur Olson

- **Olson**: Rational individuals will not act for common group interests unless the group is small or offers selective incentives (free-rider problem).
- **Olson**: Small organised lobbying groups consistently defeat the diffuse interests of millions of citizens.
- **Olson**: In politically stable democracies, interest groups accumulate over time like plaque in an artery — "institutional sclerosis."
- **Olson**: The longer a society enjoys uninterrupted political stability, the slower it grows.
- **Olson**: Post-war Germany and Japan, whose old interest-group networks were destroyed, experienced economic miracles; stable, undisrupted Britain stagnated.

## Yaneer Bar-Yam

- **Bar-Yam**: An organisation's complexity must match environmental complexity at every scale simultaneously (multi-scale law of requisite variety).
- **Bar-Yam**: With fixed resources, increasing complexity at one scale necessarily decreases it at another — a conservation law.
- **Bar-Yam**: When the FAO food price index crosses 210, social unrest becomes highly probable. He submitted this analysis to the US government on December 13, 2010 — the Tunisian fruit vendor's self-immolation that sparked the Arab Spring occurred four days later.
- **Bar-Yam**: Ethnic groups distributed in patches 20–60 km wide predict ethnic violence with 90% geographic correlation (published *Science*, September 2007; tested against India and former Yugoslavia).
- **Bar-Yam**: Technology increases environmental complexity faster than it increases organisational capacity to respond — making every new technology an accelerant for the complexity it was designed to manage.

## Peter Turchin

- **Turchin**: Human societies follow ~50-year mathematical cycles of instability, with peaks around 1870, 1920, 1970, and 2020 in the US.
- **Turchin**: He published a prediction in *Nature* (February 2010) that US instability would peak around 2020 — driven by stagnating wages, wealth gap, elite overproduction, and public debt.
- **Turchin**: Elite overproduction — producing more educated aspirants to power than existing structures can absorb — generates "counter-elites" who mobilise discontent.
- **Turchin**: American households worth $10 million or more grew from 66,000 in 1983 to 350,000 in 2010.
- **Turchin**: The Seshat Global History Databank contains ~300,000 records linked to ~400 historical polities across 1,500 variables.
- **Turchin**: A 2017 *PNAS* paper using Seshat data identified a single dimension of complexity that structures global variation in human social organisation — civilisations climb the same axis and fall off the same edge.
- **Turchin**: Instability periods historically last 10–15 years; the current period may not be over.

## Ken Hou and Houa (2025)

- **Hou / Houa**: Civilisational collapse behaves as a first-order phase transition — sudden and discontinuous, not gradual.
- **Hou / Houa**: Exact threshold formula: α_c = 1 / (1 − β), where α is feedback amplification rate and β is adaptive coupling.
- **Hou / Houa**: For systems with power-law feedback dynamics, standard early-warning indicators (developed for second-order transitions) produce no useful signals before collapse.
- **Hou / Houa**: The system performs its function right up to the threshold, then does not — no creak before the snap.

## Tainter — direct interview claims (new source: YouTube interview, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X3LP5IMWe84)

### Collapse definition (Tainter's own words)
- **Tainter**: Collapse is "the rapid simplification of a society — the rapid loss of an established level of socio-political and economic complexity," often accompanied by population loss, decreased literacy, territorial reduction, and economic simplification.
- **Tainter**: Population decline often precedes the political collapse — it functions as an early indicator of problems, not a consequence.
- **Tainter**: The word "rapid" must be defined relative to each individual society; there is no universal timescale for what counts as rapid.

### Roman Empire — additional detail
- **Tainter**: The Roman Empire was producing approximately 1 million coins per day in the 4th century — an industrial scale of production driven entirely by the need to pay the army, not to raise anyone's standard of living.
- **Tainter**: The Roman army may have doubled from approximately 300,000 permanent troops to approximately 600,000 under the reforming emperors of the late 3rd century. The Roman Empire was the only pre-modern state to maintain a standing army sufficient for all needs.
- **Tainter**: Silver debasement originated in 64 AD under Nero (from ~98% to ~90% silver); the process proved a slippery slope, continuing until ~268 AD when silver content reached approximately 2%.
- **Tainter**: Roman expansion was financed by appropriating surplus from conquered peoples; by the First Century AD Rome had reached its natural boundaries (Rhine, Danube, North Africa, the Levant) and had to sustain itself on fixed annual agricultural output — ending the growth phase.
- **Tainter**: The Eastern Roman Empire survived because it was simply wealthier — the older, more economically developed half. The East would buy off barbarians and redirect them toward the West.
- **Tainter**: The fall of Rome took several decades in the middle of the 5th century AD; 476 is a conventional date, not the moment of collapse.

### Europe's avoidance of collapse
- **Tainter**: When Napoleon invaded Russia, approximately 1 in 4 Frenchmen was in the army and another 1 in 4 was in the clergy — only half the population economically supporting the other half. Europe in the 17th–18th centuries was close to collapsing.
- **Tainter**: Europe avoided collapse through a sequence of windfalls: (1) colonial exploitation of gold and silver from the Americas and spices from the Orient; (2) discovery of coal in 18th-century England. Without these energy subsidies, collapse was plausible.

### Energy and complexity
- **Tainter**: Complexity has a metabolic/energy cost in all societies; today this cost is hidden because it is paid with fossil fuels. Ancient societies paid directly in higher taxes and harder labour.
- **Tainter**: Energy return on investment (EROI) for US oil and gas was approximately 100:1 in 1940. It is now approximately 15:1 and will continue to decline.
- **Tainter**: Declining EROI is equal in importance to climate change as a challenge to modern civilisation — it is not about running out of oil, but about the net profit of producing it declining.
- **Tainter**: Historical energy transitions take 40–50 years.
- **Tainter**: Information technology is currently in a phase of increasing returns but will inevitably reach a saturation point and then diminishing returns — as every economic transformation does.

### Innovation and diminishing returns
- **Tainter**: Innovation itself follows a diminishing-returns curve. In the 1970s, journal papers in *Science* or *Nature* typically had one author; today papers have 3–20+ authors. This reflects depletion of simple problems: it takes more and more researchers to achieve a conceptual breakthrough.
- **Tainter** (and colleagues): A study using the US Patent Office database (data 1974–~2012) found that innovation productivity declined by over 20% in a 30-year period. The metric: patents per author (output per worker) — the standard measure of economic productivity applied to innovation — fell consistently. Approximately 50% of US patents come from overseas, so the database measures global innovation.
- **Tainter**: The cost of innovation is increasing while the productivity of innovation is declining; this process will continue. Funding bodies will eventually phase out costly research areas with declining output.

### Critique of alternative theories
- **Tainter**: Ecological overshoot (Jared Diamond's *Collapse* and similar work) has "really no good evidence" — there is no case where one can demonstrate a society collapsed due to resource shortage. Tainter has written specific reviews of Diamond's reasoning.
- **Tainter**: The conventional economic argument that innovation and market forces will always solve resource shortages rests on an implicit, unacknowledged assumption that problem-solving complexity does not itself face diminishing returns. His innovation research directly falsifies this assumption.

### Meadows / Club of Rome
- **Tainter** (direct confirmation): The Club of Rome *Limits to Growth* analysis (Meadows et al., 1972) and Tainter's diminishing-returns framework are consistent — both describe increasing costs and declining output. Tainter confirmed this to Dennis Meadows personally.

### General advice and framing
- **Tainter**: "We are a species that muddles through — that is all we have ever done and all we ever will do." Humanity will continue to find its way step by step without a plan.
- **Tainter**: "Complexity itself is not necessarily a bad thing. Complexity is either useful and affordable or it isn't."
- **Tainter**: The first step toward addressing modern challenges is awareness — particularly of the energy basis of complexity.
