## 1. "Political instability may be a contributor in the coming decade" (2010, *Nature*)

**Access status:** paywalled

**Published:** *Nature* 463(7281):608, February 2010; doi:10.1038/463608a  
[Full text not retrieved — access denied (303/redirect to paywall)]

### From published descriptions and the source transcript

A short letter (correspondence) in *Nature* predicting that the United States would experience a peak of political instability around 2020. The prediction was based on two quantitative cycles identified in US history:

- A ~50-year socioeconomic cycle (stagnating real wages, widening wealth gap, exploding public debt)
- An ~50-year political instability cycle (with peaks around 1870, 1920, 1970, and — predicted — 2020)

The underlying drivers: stagnating real wages beginning in the 1970s, rising inequality, and what Turchin calls "elite overproduction" — more aspiring elites than existing power structures can absorb, generating a surplus counter-elite that mobilises popular discontent. The letter was published ten years before the events it described; the 2020 US instability peak is regarded as a successful prediction.

## 2. *Ages of Discord: A Structural-Demographic Analysis of American History* (2016, Beresta Books)

**Access status:** paywalled (book)  
**URL:** https://www.amazon.com/Ages-Discord-Peter-Turchin/dp/0996139540  
[Full text not retrieved — abstract only]

### From published descriptions and the source transcript

Full quantitative elaboration of the structural-demographic theory applied to American history. Identifies approximately 50-year instability cycles with peaks around 1870, 1920, 1970, and 2020. The model tracks four key variables: real wages, wealth inequality, state fiscal health, and elite overproduction (proxied by the number of law degrees, political aspirants, and wealthy-class growth relative to political positions available).

Introduces the concept of **elite overproduction** as the proximate mechanism: when a society produces more aspiring elites than existing power structures can absorb, the surplus becomes counter-elites who mobilise popular discontent as a path to power. This is a quantitative and empirically testable version of the mechanism Francis Bacon identified in 1625.

## 3. *End Times: Elites, Counter-Elites and the Path of Political Disintegration* (2023, Allen Lane/Penguin)

**Access status:** paywalled (book)

### From published descriptions and the source transcript

Turchin's most recent synthesis, updating and extending the Ages of Discord analysis post-2020. Warns that the current instability period (typically 10–15 years duration based on historical precedent) may not yet be over. Turchin argues the US is in the disintegrative phase of a structural-demographic cycle and identifies the conditions under which recovery or continued decline is most likely.

## 4. Turchin's publications page / Cliodynamics program overview

**Access status:** open  
**URL:** https://peterturchin.com/cliodynamics-history-as-science/

### Summary of research program

Peter Turchin founded **cliodynamics** — the application of mathematical modelling and statistical analysis to the quantitative study of historical macrosociology. The field's core method: translate verbal theories into mathematical models, then test against empirical data from the historical record.

Key resources:
- **Seshat: Global History Databank** — ~300,000 records, ~400 historical polities, ~1,500 variables; the primary empirical foundation for testing structural-demographic theory
- **Cliodynamics** (journal) — peer-reviewed publication for quantitative history
- *Secular Cycles* (with Sergey Nefedov, 2009, Princeton) — mathematical treatment of boom-bust cycles across pre-industrial states

### Methodology

Structural-demographic theory: a quantitative framework treating historical instability as an emergent property of interacting variables (wages, inequality, elite numbers, state capacity). Turchin draws explicit analogy to animal population dynamics — the field in which he trained (zoology PhD, Duke University, 1980) — arguing human societies follow similar nonlinear oscillatory patterns.
