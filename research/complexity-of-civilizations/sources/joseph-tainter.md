## 1. "Complexity, Collapse, and Sustainable Problem Solving" (2001)

**Access status:** open  
**URL:** https://digitalcommons.usu.edu/envs_facpub/1288/  
**Published in:** *Our Fragile World: Challenges and Opportunities for Sustainable Development*, EOLSS Publishers, Oxford, pp. 1803–1826.

*Note: This is a 2001 essay. The authors.md entry cites a 1996 essay titled "Complexity, Problem Solving, and Sustainable Societies." The two titles are closely related and may be different versions or the USU repository may have catalogued the 2001 update at the same URL. The core argument is consistent with the source transcript's description.*

### Core argument

Societal sustainability depends on maintaining effective problem-solving institutions. Societies are fundamentally problem-solving organisations. Early investments in institutional complexity yield high returns; as problems are solved the marginal return on further complexity declines. When new complexity costs more than it produces, the society becomes unsustainable. Collapse — a rapid reduction in complexity — is therefore not a catastrophe imposed from outside but the economically rational outcome of declining marginal returns.

### Key claims

1. Sustainability is inherently value-laden and transient rather than an objective permanent state.
2. Problem-solving institutions tend toward increasing complexity, expense, and unwieldiness over time.
3. Historical case studies across multiple civilisations show that unsustainable problem-solving trajectories reliably precede collapse.
4. Contemporary institutions can be evaluated using the same diminishing-returns framework derived from archaeological and historical evidence.
5. The path to sustainable societies runs through understanding — and limiting — unnecessary complexity accumulation.

### Methodology

Historical comparative analysis. Tainter surveys pre-industrial and ancient societies (Roman Empire, Maya, Chaco Canyon) as case studies, extracting generalisable patterns about problem-solving trajectories and their outcomes. Applied to contemporary policy.

## 2. *The Collapse of Complex Societies* (1988, Cambridge University Press)

**Access status:** paywalled (book)  
**URL:** https://www.cambridge.org/us/universitypress/subjects/archaeology/archaeological-theory-and-methods/collapse-complex-societies  
[Full text not retrieved — abstract only]

Also available as an open PDF at https://www.sustainable.soltechdesigns.com/Joseph-A-Tainter-The-collapse-of-complex-societies.pdf (third-party host; not fetched).

### From published descriptions and the source transcript

The book tests eleven conventional explanations for societal collapse (resource depletion, environmental degradation, class conflict, invasion, disease, mismanagement, etc.) against 17 civilisations on 6 continents spanning 5,000 years. All eleven explanations fail as general accounts. Tainter's alternative: collapse is a function of diminishing marginal returns on complexity investment. He notes that in an interconnected global civilisation, collapse cannot be local — it must occur as a system-wide event.

The three main case studies are the Western Roman Empire, the Classic Maya, and Chacoan society in the American Southwest.
