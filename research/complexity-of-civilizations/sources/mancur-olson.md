## 1. *The Logic of Collective Action: Public Goods and the Theory of Groups* (1965, Harvard University Press)

**Access status:** paywalled (book)  
**URL:** https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674537514  
[Full text not retrieved — abstract only]

### From published descriptions and the Econlib biography

The book identifies the **free-rider problem** as the central obstacle to collective action. Rational individuals will not voluntarily contribute to a public good because they can benefit from others' contributions without paying — so diffuse shared interests go unorganised while narrow concentrated interests succeed. This insight directly challenged pluralist political science, which assumed organised groups naturally form around shared interests.

Olson's key solution concept: groups can overcome free-riding by offering **selective incentives** — private goods (insurance, professional services, publications) available only to members. The lobbying activities of large associations are thus a *by-product* of their service functions, not their primary driver.

## 2. *The Rise and Decline of Nations: Economic Growth, Stagflation, and Social Rigidities* (1982, Yale University Press)

**Access status:** paywalled (book)  
**URL:** https://www.amazon.com/Rise-Decline-Nations-Stagflation-Rigidities/dp/0300030797  
[Full text not retrieved — abstract only]

### From published descriptions and the Econlib biography

The book extends *The Logic of Collective Action* to macroeconomic history. Its central claim: in any society that enjoys prolonged political stability, small organised interest groups ("distributional coalitions") accumulate over time. Each coalition lobbies for concentrated benefits at diffuse public cost. As the density of these coalitions increases, the economy becomes **sclerotic** — growth slows, rigidities spread, and the society loses the capacity to adapt.

### Key claims

1. **Stability breeds sclerosis:** The longer a society enjoys uninterrupted political stability, the more distributional coalitions it accumulates and the slower its growth rate.
2. **Post-war miracles explained:** Germany and Japan, whose pre-war interest-group networks were destroyed, could rebuild without institutional drag — explaining their rapid post-war growth. Britain, whose networks survived intact, stagnated.
3. **Coalition logic:** Distributional coalitions rationally seek to reduce competition and increase their members' shares rather than expand the total pie.
4. **Prediction:** Nations with "encompassing" organisations (unions representing the whole workforce, not just a craft) have less sclerosis than those with fragmented narrow coalitions, because encompassing groups internalise some of the costs they impose on the economy.

### Methodology

Comparative economic history. Olson tests his institutional-sclerosis thesis against cross-national growth data and historical case studies, including detailed treatment of post-war Europe.

## 3. Econlib biographical entry

**Access status:** open  
**URL:** https://www.econlib.org/library/enc/bios/olson.html

### Summary

Mancur Olson (1932–1998), North Dakota farm-raised economist with a Harvard PhD (1963). Taught at Maryland from 1969 until his death. His core contributions span collective action theory, institutional sclerosis, and governance quality. His posthumous *Power and Prosperity* (2000) distinguished between "stationary autocrats" (with long-term stakes in prosperity) and "roving autocrats" (short-term predators), developing a theory of how political structure shapes economic incentives. Olson died in 1998 mid-project, leaving his most ambitious work unfinished.
