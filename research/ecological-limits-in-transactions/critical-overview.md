# Critical Overview — Ecological Limits in Transactions

**Inquiry:** "Ecological limits will remain politically weak and economically secondary until they are built into transactions themselves rather than handled afterward by taxes, standards, and reporting."

**Source:** Valdsalu & Aaslaid (2026). Single.Earth Foundation Working Paper No. 1.

---

## What the evidence supports

The originating statement captures a structural observation that is theoretically coherent
and partially supported by the empirical record. The distinction between ex ante and ex post
instruments is real and consequential. An instrument that embeds an ecological constraint
at the point of transaction operates before the economic decision is made; an instrument
that adjusts prices or imposes costs afterward operates after the decision. This is a
meaningful difference in mechanism design, not just in calibration.

The EU ETS record provides the most direct empirical test. Carbon prices were low through
most of Phase 1 and Phase 2 (2005–2012), largely due to over-allocation of permits and
later to the 2008 economic contraction. The instrument was substantially inactive during
these periods. Recovery after the Market Stability Reserve activation in 2019 and the
2021–2022 price surge produced measurable emissions reductions in covered sectors, but the
episode also confirmed that ex post price instruments require sustained political commitment
to calibration to remain effective. When political conditions change, the constraint weakens.

Daly (1996) and Dasgupta (2021) provide the theoretical foundation: natural capital is
structurally underpriced because ownership, measurement, and political representation are
weak, and price signals that attempt to correct this externality after the fact compete
with a market logic that operates in real time at the point of transaction.

## Where the claim overstates

The strongest version of the claim — that ex post instruments *cannot* work — is harder to
defend than the claim that they have been insufficient at current calibration and scope.
Several environmental harms have been substantially reduced through regulation: ozone
depletion was addressed by the Montreal Protocol, leaded petrol was eliminated across most
jurisdictions, acid rain was significantly reduced in Europe and North America. These
examples do not involve transaction-level embedding. They involved regulatory standards,
international agreements, and in some cases taxes.

The paper's own mechanism design also has features that would be subject to political
economy pressures analogous to those affecting carbon prices. The calibration parameter α
must be set and adjusted by some institution. If that institution can be lobbied to keep α
low, the mechanism's ex ante character does not protect it from the same political weakness
the originating statement attributes to ex post instruments. The paper acknowledges that
"the choice of adjustment rule is a central governance question" without fully tracing its
implications.

The measurement constraint is also more significant than the structural argument implies.
If ecological intensity coefficients E_i can only be estimated at coarse sector level with
high within-sector variance, the practical difference between a transaction-level burn and
a sector-level tax may be small. The mechanism's advantage depends on the granularity of
measurement that can be achieved — and the paper acknowledges this is currently limited.

## Where the record is contested

The interpretation of ETS performance divides analysts. Some read the periods of low
carbon prices as evidence of instrument failure; others read them as evidence of political
miscalibration that could in principle be corrected within the same instrument category.
The Market Stability Reserve was itself a design correction applied within the existing
framework. Whether that demonstrates the instrument's adaptability or its fragility is a
matter of interpretation.

The planetary boundaries framing, on which the paper partly relies, is also contested.
The specific boundary values are the product of judgment calls about acceptable risk,
and critics have argued that the framework conflates global averages with local impacts
in ways that matter for policy design.

## Key unresolved questions

1. Whether α governance is any more politically durable than carbon price governance.
2. Whether sector-level ecological intensity estimates are sufficient to produce
   meaningfully different outcomes from sector-level taxes.
3. Whether the distributional effects of transaction-level ecological burns would
   produce political economy problems comparable to or worse than existing instruments.
4. How the mechanism would interact with credit creation — the paper flags this but
   does not analyze it.

## Implication for the public article

The public article should state clearly that the originating statement captures a real
structural concern but overstates it as a universal claim. The more defensible position
is that ex post instruments are *structurally vulnerable* to being made economically
inconsequential — not that they cannot work under any governance conditions. The paper's
mechanism design is a coherent response to this vulnerability, not a proven solution.
